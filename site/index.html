θ-Aware

The angle-dependent error of IBM's fractional RZZ(θ) gate, and what a compiler could do with it.

Elbaraa Saad and Eyad Abdelraoof · Measured on ibm_marrakesh and ibm_kingston (Heron)

Press → to begin · drag the chip to rotate it

Introduce yourself. One sentence: IBM's processors have a gate that rotates by any angle θ, IBM tells us one error number for every angle, and I measured whether that is true.

The problem

One number for every angle

Heron processors can apply RZZ(θ) directly, for any 0 < θ ≤ π/2, instead of building it from two CZ gates.

IBM's documentation says the error it reports for this gate is just a copy of the non-fractional gate's counterpart (which may not be the same), and Pauli twirling is not supported, so coherent errors are not averaged away.

A compiler that only reads the reported number cannot tell a good angle from a bad one.

Three questions: Does the error depend on θ? What is it made of? How much could a compiler gain by knowing it?

The dashed line is what IBM reports for pair (10, 11). The dots are what I measured on the same pair. Same gate, same day.

How the gate acts

RZZ(θ) turns one qubit by ±θ

Put the second qubit in |+⟩ (on the equator). If the first qubit is |0⟩, RZZ(θ) turns it by +θ around Z; if |1⟩, by −θ.

A real gate turns a little too far, δ(θ), and adds its own single-qubit phase β(θ). Both depend on the angle.

ideal measured gate

Drag the angle down to π/24. On (10, 11) the gate turns by about 0.41 rad when we ask for 0.13: it cannot rotate that little. Increase N: the error grows N times. That is the phase-amplification idea on the next slides.

Gate model

Four numbers per angle, not one

RZZ(θ + δ) · RZa(α) · RZb(β) · then error ε
  • ε(θ) incoherent error: the random part. Measured with mirror circuits.
  • δ(θ) over-rotation: the gate turns too far or too little.
  • α(θ), β(θ) local Z phases on each qubit. Invisible to IBM's reported figure.

δ, α and β are measured with phase amplification: repeat the gate N = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 times so a small error grows until it can be read.

Everything coherent can be corrected for free with virtual Z gates. Only ε is a true loss.

Measuring ε(θ)

The mirror: go out, come back

Apply d layers of RZZ(θ), then d inverse layers (X·RZZ(θ)·X, since the hardware takes no negative angles). A perfect gate comes home exactly.

What does not come home is the random error. Survival falls as S(d) = A·p2d for d = 1, 4, 8, 16, 32, and ε = (15/16)(1 − p).

Turn δ on: the path drifts away from the ideal one, but still comes home. The mirror is blind to over-rotation, which is why δ needs its own measurement.

In simulation I injected δ = 0.02 rad and the mirror did not see it. That is a feature: the mirror isolates ε. But it also means mirrored circuits hide coherent errors, so they must not be used alone to evaluate the compiler.

Result 2 · measured

A hidden phase is the bigger coherent error

Correcting only δ made results worse (median −0.9%). Re-reading the data showed why: a large single-qubit phase that the δ measurement cancels by design.

On (10, 11) the local phases reach α −0.26…+0.18 and β −0.19…+0.28 rad per gate. Elsewhere about 0.06 rad or less.

pair (10, 11) other marrakesh pairs

I kept the negative result, because it led to the main finding. (10, 11) was included on purpose: the pilot flagged it, so it is not a typical pair.

Result 3 · measured

Correcting it is free, and helps unevenly

Virtual RZ(−α), RZ(−β) after each gate: no extra error, no extra time. Test: from |++⟩, apply RZZ(θ) K times with Kθ = π or 2π, and read the ideal output.

+1.0%median gain, marrakesh, p = 5.7×10−8 (98 cases)
+0.6%median gain, kingston, p = 6.1×10−6 (98 cases)

Most of the gain sits on a few bad pairs. On (0, 1), whose calibration was erratic, the correction hurt. A real compiler needs a calibration-quality check.

Each dot is one test case. Above the diagonal, the correction helped. On (10, 11) several jump from 0.06–0.41 to 0.87–0.96, but three collapse to about 0.02: at π/12 and π/8 the gate cannot rotate that little.

Result 4 · measured

Choosing the implementation can matter more

On the worst pair at small angles, the standard decomposition with two CZ gates reached 0.86–0.89 success. The fractional gate reached 0.05–0.44.

IBM reports the same error for both.

One pair, chosen because it is bad, at its worst angles. 2,000 shots each.

2-CZ uses twice as many two-qubit gates and still wins here. This is the strongest argument for a compiler that knows per-angle, per-pair behaviour.

The θ-Aware compiler · built

One decision per gate

For every RZZ(θ), θ-Aware picks the cheapest of: the fractional gate, the fractional gate + free virtual RZ corrections (optionally with a pre-distorted angle), or 2 × CZ, using the measured error for that pair at that angle. It can also choose which pairs to use.

fractional RZZ on a bad pair 2 × CZ virtual RZ (free)

The 3D scene replays the earlier simulated rule (8-qubit lines); the next two slides show the built compiler.

Nearly all of the projected gain comes from circuits that touch a bad pair. On circuits that avoid them the gain is about 0.4–0.6%. So H2's verdict depends on how common bad pairs are.

Result 5 · measured outcomes, replayed

Tested on real past jobs

We ran the compiler on the exact circuits of earlier hardware jobs, gave it only the calibrations that existed before each job, and looked up how its choice actually did on the QPU.

On the bad pair it chose 2-CZ every time: success 0.23 → 0.88, 6.4× fewer errors. It chose the best measured option in 95 of 102 held-out cases.

Honest limit: on kingston's healthy pairs it changed nothing measurable (0.879 vs 0.880). E10 is 4 cases on one pair.

E4 was used to fit the one parameter κ; E6 and E10 are held out. Re-running with κ from 0 to 1 changed no E10 decision. "Best" means within two standard errors (2,000 shots).

Projection · simulation

Fewer errors and fewer gates

120 algorithm circuits on 6 qubits, with the measured gate parameters and measured-size drift.

  • 93% fewer errors than Qiskit with fractional gates (worse on 0% of circuits).
  • 64% fewer errors than Qiskit without them, using 45% fewer two-qubit gates.
  • With layout choice: 80% fewer errors than 2-CZ at half the gates.

Projection, not data. At 18× the measured drift the edge over 2-CZ shrinks; day-scale drift is not measured yet.

Metric: state fidelity before measurement, so every error counts. Single-qubit gates and readout are ideal in the simulation; crosstalk and leakage are not modelled. On circuits without a bad pair the gain over fractional gates is smaller and was not confirmed by the kingston replay.

Projection

Gain grows with depth, and with bad pairs

Median relative gain of the simulated θ-aware rule over fractional gates with no correction, by number of layers. The dashed line is the plan's 10% target (H2).

With bad pairs avoided, the gain falls below 1%. A layout pass that simply avoids bad pairs would capture most of it, but finding them still needs per-angle data.

Metric: state fidelity times gate survival, not the hardware's success probability. 64% of simulated circuits touch a bad pair, which over-represents them.

Where the hypotheses stand

Expected results of the full experiment

H1 · likely

Constant error rejected for ≥ 50% of pairs.

Projected 63% with 60 pairs (95% range 43–75%); 91% of resamples reach 50%.

H2 · promising, open

θ-aware compilation improves median success by ≥ 10%.

Built and tested: 6.4× fewer errors on the bad pair (replay); no measurable gain on healthy pairs. Needs hardware.

H3 · uncertain

The θ-aware model ranks circuits better than IBM's errors.

ρ 0.74 vs 0.53, difference CI [−0.07, +0.42] includes 0.

H4 · plausible

Same CI width on ε with ≤ 50% of the QPU time.

Two depths {1, 32} at 1,106 shots: 43% of the time.

Exploratory: one day, two devices, 8 pairs each, 551 QPU seconds. Several design choices were made after seeing the data.

Be honest with the judges: nothing here is confirmatory. The next step is to pre-register the corrected design.

Next

What comes next

  1. Pre-register the corrected design: pairs chosen without the pilot, a fixed rule for bad pairs, a likelihood test for H1.
  2. Multi-day sweep: ≥ 30 pairs per device, 3 days, about 18 QPU minutes.
  3. Run the compiler on hardware against Qiskit (it is built: phase correction, 2-CZ choice, bad-pair-aware layout, dry run first).
  4. Evaluate on ≥ 40 circuits with classically computable outputs, not only mirrors.

Main message so far: a single reported error misdescribes fractional RZZ(θ) gates, and a few pairs that look normal on paper can be far worse than the standard decomposition.
