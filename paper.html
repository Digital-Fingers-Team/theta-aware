θ-Aware: Measuring the Angle-Dependent Error of Fractional RZZ(θ) Gates on Superconducting Hardware, and What a Compiler Could Do with It

Elbaraa Saad and Eyad Abdelraoof · ISEF category: Software Design (SFTD), Algorithms · Working paper, 12 November 2026
Status: exploratory results from one day of hardware runs; the θ-Aware compiler, built and tested on recorded and simulated data; model-based projections. The compiler has not yet run on hardware, and the confirmatory experiment has not been run.
Abstract. IBM's fractional two-qubit gate RZZ(θ) reports one error value for every angle, which IBM documents as a copy of the non-fractional gate's error. We measured the gate as a function of θ on two Heron processors in ten experiments on 14 October 2026, using 551 QPU seconds. Mirror circuits show that a constant incoherent error is rejected (Holm-corrected, α = 0.01) in a median of 16 of 24 pair-sweeps (three sweeps of 8 pairs; 14–18 across bootstrap seeds). Phase-amplification circuits reveal an angle-dependent single-qubit Z phase, invisible to IBM's reported figure, reaching about 0.3 rad per gate on the worst pair. Removing it with virtual RZ gates raised success significantly across 196 test cases, but the median gain is small (+1.0% and +0.6% on the two devices): most of the gain sits on a few bad pairs, and the correction hurt the one pair with an unstable calibration. On the worst pair at small angles, the standard two-CZ decomposition reached 0.86–0.89 success against 0.05–0.44 for the fractional gate, although both are reported with the same error. We then built θ-Aware, a Qiskit compiler pass that chooses, for every RZZ(θ), between the fractional gate, the fractional gate with free phase corrections, and the standard two-CZ decomposition, from the measured per-angle model. Replayed on the circuits of past hardware jobs with only the calibrations that existed before each job, it chose the best measured implementation (within two standard errors) in 95 of 102 held-out cases. On the bad pair it raised success from 0.23 to 0.88, 6.4 times fewer errors; on the other device's healthy pairs it changed nothing measurable (0.879 vs 0.880). In simulation of 120 algorithm circuits with the measured parameters and measured-size drift, it made 93% fewer errors than Qiskit with fractional gates and 64% fewer than Qiskit without them, while using 45% fewer two-qubit gates than the latter. None of this is confirmatory: the compiler results are retrospective or simulated, and hardware validation awaits QPU access. Projections of the full experiment suggest H1 is likely to hold, H4 is plausible and H3 is uncertain.

1. Introduction

Fractional gates let IBM's Heron processors apply an RZZ(θ) rotation directly, with 0 < θ ≤ π/2, instead of building it from two CZ gates [1]. They have been used at large scale, including benchmarks on up to 156 qubits [2]. They also come with a blind spot. IBM's documentation says the error reported for a fractional gate "is just a copy of the non-fractional gate's counterpart (which may not be the same)", that the transpiler has "limited capability" to use RZZ(θ), and that Pauli twirling is not supported with these gates [1]. Without twirling, coherent errors are not converted into stochastic noise, so they stay in the circuit and can add up.

A compiler that reads only the reported number therefore cannot tell a good angle from a bad one, or a good implementation from a bad one. We did not find published work that measures RZZ(θ) error and its coherent parts as functions of θ on hardware, or a compiler that uses such data, although our search was not exhaustive. This project asks three questions: does the error really depend on θ, what does the error consist of, and how much could a compiler gain by knowing it?

Hypotheses from the research plan

  • H1. The constant-error model is rejected (Holm-corrected α = 0.01) for at least 50% of qubit pairs.
  • H2. θ-Aware compilation improves median success probability by at least 10% over Qiskit level 3 with fractional gates (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, paired by circuit).
  • H3. Fidelity predicted from the θ-Aware model ranks circuits better than predictions from IBM-reported errors (Spearman ρ; bootstrap 95% CI of the difference excludes 0).
  • H4. An optimized measurement design reaches the same confidence-interval width on ε(θ) as a uniform shot allocation with at most 50% of the QPU seconds.
How to read this paper. Blue boxes and Section 3 are measurements. Orange boxes and Section 6 are projections: outputs of a model built on those measurements. No projected number was measured on hardware. All measurements come from one day, and several of the study's design choices were made after seeing them, so they motivate the hypotheses and cannot confirm them.

2. Methods

Hardware and budget. ibm_marrakesh and ibm_kingston, under the IBM Quantum Open Plan (10 QPU minutes per rolling 28 days). Software: Python 3.12, Qiskit 2.5.2 [3], qiskit-ibm-runtime 0.50.0. All ten experiments (E1–E10) used 551 of 600 seconds on one account. ibm_fez also passed the access check but was not measured.

Gate model. We model one physical gate as RZZ(θ + δ) · RZa(α) · RZb(β), followed by incoherent error ε. Each of δ, α, β and ε may depend on θ.

Incoherent error ε(θ). Mirror circuits [4] apply d layers of RZZ(θ) and then d inverse layers, each inverse implemented as X·RZZ(θ)·X because the hardware does not accept negative angles. Survival is fitted as S(d) = A·p2d for d = 1, 4, 8, 16, 32, and ε = (15/16)(1 − p). A simulation with an injected δ = 0.02 rad confirmed that the mirror is blind to over-rotation.

Coherent errors δ, α, β. One qubit is prepared in |0⟩ or |1⟩ and the other in |+⟩, RZZ(θ) is repeated N = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 times in the same direction, and the second qubit's phase is read in the X and Y bases, in the spirit of phase-amplification calibration [5]. The difference of the two control settings gives θ + δ, and their sum gives the local phase. Swapping roles gives the other qubit's phase. Simulation recovered an injected δ of 0.02 rad as 0.0195–0.0200.

Correctness test. From |++⟩, apply RZZ(θ) K times with Kθ = π (ideal output |11⟩) or Kθ = 2π (ideal |00⟩), then H⊗H. Success is the probability of the ideal output. Variants: raw; local, with virtual RZ(−α), RZ(−β) after each gate [6], which adds no error or time; local+δ, which also pre-distorts the angle; and 2-CZ, the standard decomposition.

Safeguards. Circuits are transpiled at optimization level 0 with barriers, and a check aborts submission if any RZZ was merged or decomposed. Circuit order is randomized with a fixed seed, so drift cannot look like angle dependence. Disjoint pairs run in parallel in one circuit.

Statistics. Parametric bootstrap with 300 resamples; constant-error test by χ² against the weighted mean, with Holm correction [7] across pairs (α = 0.01). Correction tests use a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test [8]. Because the χ² weights come from the bootstrap, we repeated the H1 analysis over 40 seeds. The plan specifies a likelihood-ratio test; the χ² test here is a stand-in for it and must be replaced or justified before pre-registration.

3.2 A hidden, angle-dependent phase is the larger coherent error

Our first correction (E3) targeted only δ and did not help: median relative gain −0.9% over 92 cases, and worse on pair (10, 11). We kept this negative result because it led to the main finding. The differential δ measurement cancels any single-qubit phase by design, and re-reading the same data showed a large one. A dedicated calibration (E4) gave α from −0.26 to +0.18 and β from −0.19 to +0.28 rad per gate on (10, 11); elsewhere the phases were about 0.06 rad or less (Figure 2).

3.5 Coherent errors looked stable for an hour; ε(θ) did not

Repeating the marrakesh sweep about an hour later, δ(θ) correlated at r ≥ 0.83 between runs, changing by at most 0.018 rad. The correlation of ε(θ) ranged from -0.10 to 0.79. On (10, 11), ε at θ = 0.13 fell from 0.0086 to 0.0006. This suggests calibrating coherent errors once per session and correcting them while treating ε as time-varying. One hour says nothing about stability over days, which is what a usable compiler needs.

3.6 The measured gate model predicts better, but not significantly so

Using each pair's calibration to predict 196 raw test outcomes it never saw: Spearman ρ = 0.74 (mean absolute error 0.071) against ρ = 0.53 (0.126) for IBM's reported error. The difference is +0.21, with a 95% pair-clustered bootstrap CI of [-0.07, +0.42], which includes 0. Without the two bad pairs the advantage disappears: 0.64 against 0.67, CI [-0.13, +0.07]. Only 14 pairs inform this interval.

4. The θ-Aware compiler (built; tested on recorded and simulated data)

4.1 What it does

θ-Aware is a Qiskit pass (package thetaaware/). After Qiskit lays out and routes a circuit with fractional RZZ allowed, the pass rewrites every RZZ(θ). It first folds θ into the hardware's range [0, π/2] with exact identities: a negative angle becomes X·RZZ(|θ|)·X on one qubit, and |θ| > π/2 splits off RZZ(π), which equals Z⊗Z up to phase and is virtual. It then picks one of four implementations for that physical pair: the fractional gate as it is (what Qiskit does with fractional gates on); the fractional gate followed by virtual RZ(−α), RZ(−β), which costs nothing; the same with the requested angle pre-distorted so that θ + δ lands on θ, when the measured response is monotonic; or the standard two-CZ decomposition (what Qiskit does with fractional gates off).

Each option gets a predicted error from the measured model: ε(θ), plus m times the average gate infidelity of the coherent error the option leaves, where m counts how often the circuit repeats that pair and angle, because coherent errors add in amplitude. Two-CZ costs twice IBM's reported error. The cheapest option wins; ties go to fewer two-qubit gates. Gates on pairs with no calibration stay as Qiskit would leave them. A layout score adds up the best option's cost over a candidate placement, so the compiler can also choose which pairs to use. One parameter, κ, the share of a measured coherent error that survives correction, was fitted on E4 alone and came out at κ = 0.00, the edge of its range. We therefore re-ran every held-out test for κ from 0 to 1: E10's choices did not change, and E6's mean success stayed between 0.880 and 0.884. Median compile time was 429 ms per 6-qubit circuit on a 2-core server.

4.2 Correctness

254 automated tests pass, with none failing. On an ideal device, every option equals RZZ(θ) up to global phase for 50 angles from −2π to 2π, including 0, ±π/2, ±π and 2π. Every angle sent to the hardware lies in [0, π/2]. With the measured model as the device, the corrections cancel it exactly for all 8 marrakesh pairs, both qubit orders and five angles. Compiled random circuits equal their originals, and the simulator reproduces Section 3.6's prediction formula to within 10−6.

6. Expected results of the full experiment (projections)

Projection, not data
Everything in this section is a model output, computed with no QPU use by 17_paper_analysis.py. Projections carry forward the limits of a one-day sample in which one marrakesh pair was deliberately included because it looked bad.

6.1 H1: likely to hold

We resampled the measured per-pair p-values to the plan's size and re-applied Holm across all pairs. With 60 pairs, the projected rejected share has a median of 63% (95% range 43%–75%), and 91% of resamples reach the 50% threshold. With 30 pairs: 63% and 95%. These figures use one seed's p-values, so they carry the seed sensitivity described in Section 3.1. Risks: resampling assumes new pairs behave like these 24 sweeps, repeated sweeps share pairs, and a multi-day analysis with day as a random effect may behave differently.

What we expect
Gains should be large on bad pairs and tiny elsewhere (about 0.4%–0.6% on circuits that avoid them), growing with depth. The median crosses 10% only if over half of the circuits touch a bad pair. On a line of 8 qubits that takes a bad-pair share of about 9% per edge, falling to 5% at 16 qubits and 2.2% at 32. Our only bad pair not chosen in advance is (0, 1): 1 of 14 unforced pairs, about 7%, on far too small a sample to settle it. So H2's verdict is open, and larger circuits should favour it. A layout pass that simply avoids bad pairs would capture most of the gain. That still needs per-angle data, because IBM's reported numbers do not reveal these pairs, and it may be the project's most practical outcome. The Qiskit level 3 baseline may also do better or worse than the plain fractional baseline simulated here.

6.3 H3: uncertain

The measured preview favours the model on average, but its interval includes 0 and the advantage comes from bad pairs (Section 3.6). The evaluation's circuit mix will decide this. With few bad pairs, H3 may well fail, which would itself be useful: it would mean the reported error ranks ordinary pairs about as well as the measured model on circuits of this type.

6.4 H4: plausible, with a cost

Using a cost model fitted to two of our jobs (T ≈ 0.102 s per circuit + 0.172 ms per shot), we simulated the depth and shot allocation needed to match the CI width of the current five-depth design at 1,000 shots, for ε from 0.0003 to 0.008. Two depths {1, 32} at 1,106 shots need 43% of the QPU time. Two depths {1, 96} at 558 shots need 29%, but extrapolate beyond any depth we have measured. Two depths cannot test whether the decay is exponential, and per-shot time will grow with depth in ways this model ignores, so we would keep a small number of intermediate depths as a check. The plan's proofs remain to be written.

6.5 Budget

From the measured 0.27 s per 1,000-shot circuit, two devices over three days need about 492 s of sweeps and 459 s of phase calibration, plus 142 s of evaluation (40 circuits × 4 variants × 2 devices at 2,000 shots): about 18 minutes, before any savings from H4. That is roughly two full 10-minute allowances, and the project's current account has 49 s left until its window rolls forward. Thirty pairs per device add no QPU time if they share circuits, but more parallel pairs may add crosstalk, which needs checking.

6.6 Design changes this work suggests before pre-registration

  1. Do not evaluate H2 only with mirrored circuits. The plan runs larger circuits in mirrored form. In the gate model, conjugating with X on one qubit reverses the ZZ rotation and that qubit's local phase, so δ and α cancel while β adds up. A mirror therefore hides much of the coherent error this project is about. Use circuits with a classically computable output instead.
  2. Fix a rule for bad pairs in advance, both for excluding unmeasurable pairs and for the calibration-quality gate.
  3. Choose pairs without looking at the pilot. Pair (10, 11) must not be forced into the confirmatory sample, or must be analysed apart from it.
  4. Replace or justify the H1 test, so the verdict does not depend on bootstrap seed; use more resamples or an analytic likelihood.

6.7 What would change our conclusions

If the multi-day data show δ, α and β drifting between days, per-session calibration costs too much to be free. If bad pairs are rare or short-lived, the compiler's value shrinks to almost nothing on typical circuits, and the main contribution becomes the dataset and the warning about reported errors. If IBM begins reporting per-angle errors, the measurement half of this project loses its practical purpose.

7. Limitations

  • One day, two devices, few pairs. All of it is exploratory, and hypotheses and methods evolved while we looked at the data.
  • Selection. Pair (10, 11) was forced in because the pilot flagged it. It supplies the most striking results, so those results are not typical.
  • Simple test circuits. The correctness tests repeat one gate on isolated pairs, while real circuits mix angles and neighbours. A gain here does not carry over automatically.
  • Simplified simulation. It has no crosstalk, readout error or layout search, assumes a line topology, and treats incoherent error as depolarizing.
  • Compiler evidence is retrospective or simulated. The replay can only score options a past job measured, and its decisive result is four cases on one pair. The simulation uses the same gate model as the compiler, perturbed by drift, so it cannot reveal errors the model lacks, such as crosstalk, leakage or the unexplained π/3 failure.
  • Incomplete gate model. The π/3, K = 3 failure on (10, 11) is unexplained, and one pair's calibration was untrustworthy.
  • Analysis change. The H1 count moves with the bootstrap seed. The log's single value of 5/8 is the median for that sweep, and one run of this paper's analysis gave 6/8; we report the range (Section 3.1).

8. Conclusion

On the data so far, a single reported error misdescribes fractional RZZ(θ) gates: error depends on the angle for most measured pairs, and a coherent phase that IBM does not report can be large and can be removed for free. The sharpest practical message comes from a small number of bad pairs that look normal in reported calibration data but can perform far worse than the standard decomposition. The θ-Aware compiler now turns this into a decision: it detects when the fractional gate is the wrong choice and switches, which on the bad pair we measured turned 0.23 into 0.88 success, while keeping the fractional gate's lower two-qubit gate count elsewhere. On healthy pairs the recorded data show no measurable gain, so its value on hardware depends on how common bad pairs are and how fast calibrations drift. The next steps are to pre-register the corrected design, run the multi-day sweep, and run the compiler against Qiskit on hardware.
